---
title: In Pursuit of Spring Part 2
date: 2026-07-06
---

In early June 2026 three friends and I set off on a four day cycle tour from East London to Bath via the Surrey Hills, New Forest and Salisbury Plain. The route was loosely based on Edward Thomas’ 1914 novel In Pursuit of Spring in which he documented a cycling pilgrimage from London to the Quantock Hills over the Easter weekend. Our journey followed in general direction and certainly more in spirit rather than in faithful adherence to his precise route.

Hailing from East Anglia, the journey started with a bright and early train to Stratford on a Friday morning at the end of what had been a very wet week indeed, but in the hope of the weather brightening as we headed south-west. The four of us rode across London following canal towpaths and passing under the Thames to emerge in Greenwich. Our journey really began where Thomas did; outside his house at 61 Shelgate Rd, Clapham, of course marked by a blue plaque. As we were getting our tourist snaps, a gentleman from number 63 struck up conversation wanting to know why he’d see the occasional band of cyclists taking selfies outside his neighbour’s house. We explained the connection to the novel, the great war poet, our journey and our search of spring. He’d never thought to look at the blue plaque but was thrilled to learn about his erstwhile neighbour’s great works.

We said our goodbyes and began the journey we’d come for. Compasses pointed south-westerly we followed the almost dead straight A24 through Tooting, Merton and Morden until it became the Epsom Road. In his book, Edward Thomas describes this part of the journey as the point of change between city and country, passing a mix of market gardens and newly developed suburbs. The author probably wouldn’t have recognised the landscape we rode through, with enormous change and development sprawling out in the inter-war period. Sadly, Edward Thomas was killed in action at Arras in 1917 at the age of 39. We on the other hand felt much the same feeling he describes of leaving the smoke behind as everything became noticeably greener and leafier and the air clearer, the closer we got to Ewell and Epsom.

The first bit of real “rough stuff” we encountered was as we joined Stane Street, just before crossing the M25 on a byway. This arrow-straight Roman road starts as a relatively innocuous footpath, but very quickly becomes a series of steep ups and downs made up of rutted chalk and flint with an arm battering final 1000m down a gulley to the base of Box Hill. No more than 3 miles in total, the harshness and level of concentration required meant we welcomed the smooth tarmac snaking up above Dorking. By the time we reached the top opinion had changed somewhat! We were rewarded though with a spectacular view Southward and the magnificent run down into town for a cup of tea and a bite to eat. 

Out of Dorking we headed west and up a bridleway on to a ridge line which we followed for another ten miles or so affording us more spectacular views across the Surrey Hills before dropping down into Guildford. Across the four days we would find ourselves in moments of peace; in a meadow, on a moor, following a chalk ridge or standing on a bridge listening to a chalk stream flow beneath us. One of us would declare it a moment in pursuit of spring in homage to the spirit of Thomas’ intentions. We found one of these moments on that ridge in a meadow of wildflowers, completely at peace and with an unrestricted view of the countryside rolling below us. Like others I’m sure, I often get asked why I would spend my free time flogging myself up steep hills, across unmade tracks or fording streams just to end up in the same place. This is why, and it’s impossible to describe - it must be experienced.

Towards the end of our first day we joined the river Wey and followed it to Farnham Brewery, nestled in the valley a few miles south of town. The brewery has a tap serving their own ales and lagers as well as a pizza van from which we hoovered up what was on offer before the final push to Aldershot for the night. The first day of our trip felt very much like the first chapters of In Pursuit of Spring - pedalling away to escape the hustle and bustle of town and finding occasional moments of peace in the hills. But like Thomas we’d struggled to really break free of the grip of London, that would come in the following days.
